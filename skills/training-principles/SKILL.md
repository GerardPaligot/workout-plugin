# General principles of strength training

## Progressive overload: not just load

Progressive overload is the engine of progress, but it is not limited to adding weight to the bar.

### Forms of progressive overload

- Increase the **load** (the most obvious).
- Improve the **execution technique** (often underestimated and very effective).
- Increase the number of **reps** at equal load.
- Increase the number of **sets**.
- Reduce the **rest times** (use with caution).

### Concrete example

Reducing the load to improve technique (e.g. going from 50 kg (110 lb) to 38 kg (84 lb) on the one-arm dumbbell row with flawless execution) can produce **better results** for hypertrophy than forcing a load that is too heavy with poor execution.

## Ego: the enemy of progress

Ego pushes you to lift loads greater than what you can actually control with good technique.

### Consequences of ego lifting

- Bad lever arm → reduced stimulation of the target muscle.
- Excessive compensation by other muscles (biceps, forearms, erector spinae...).
- Increased risk of injury.
- Stagnating progress despite "impressive" loads.

### Recommendation

- Accept reducing the loads to improve execution.
- Assess movement quality against the biomechanical criteria (see skill `lifting-biomechanics`).
- Mechanical tension + progressive overload (technique included) > heavy loads poorly executed.

## Exercise economy

Multiplying exercises for the same muscle does not mean better results.

### Principles

- Identify the **primary actions** of the target muscle.
- Cover these actions with a **minimum of well-chosen exercises**.
- 2 exercises with 3-4 sets each are often enough for a muscle group.
- Excess exercises generate **useless volume** (waste) which:
  - Negatively impacts **recovery**.
  - Prevents training as **frequently** as possible.
  - **Limits** overall progress.

### Exercise selection: criteria

1. Cover the different tensions (stretch + contraction) across the whole range of motion.
2. Choose the exercises where you feel the target muscle best.
3. Choose the exercises that allow regular progressive overload.
4. Don't get emotionally attached to an exercise (e.g. deadlift) if it doesn't serve the hypertrophy goal.
5. Check that each component of the movement truly corresponds to an action of the target muscle (see muscle fibers in skill `lifting-biomechanics`).

### Humerus rotations: useless during exercises

Humerus rotations (internal or external rotation) during an exercise add **no extra stimulation**, whatever the muscle group (chest, deltoids, back...).

- Choose a rotation position **before** starting the set and **keep it fixed** throughout.
- Benefits: (1) avoid shoulder pain, (2) make the exercise more effective.
- Exercises concerned: all presses, all pulls.

### Hand grip (pronation / supination / neutral): choose and keep

Grip does **not significantly change** the stimulation of the target muscle in most cases:

- **Lats**: pronation ≈ supination ≈ neutral (no difference in development).
- **Triceps**: supination is **disadvantageous** (the triceps is not responsible for supination, weaker grip, reduced loads). Prefer pronation or neutral grip.
- For biceps: supination is an action of the biceps, so it is relevant.
- **Rear deltoid**: the **neutral grip** (slight external rotation) may be more effective than pronation (EMG study). Test both.

Recommendation: choose the grip with which you (1) feel the muscle best, (2) can use the heaviest loads, and (3) keep it. Don't vary grips needlessly.

### Non-essential exercises

Some popular exercises are **not essential** for hypertrophy and can be replaced by better alternatives:

- An exercise can be dropped if it brings nothing more than the other exercises in the program.
- If an exercise causes joint pain in most lifters, it is better to avoid it.
- Examples: conventional deadlift (for the lats), Arnold press (for the shoulders), upright row (for the shoulders), dumbbell kickback (for the triceps), close-grip bench press (for the triceps), drag curl (for the biceps), EZ-bar curl (for the biceps), squeeze press (for the chest), standing cable crossover (for the chest), circular leg press (for the quadriceps), linear leg press (for the quadriceps), T-bar row (for the lats).

### Criteria for evaluating an exercise

To decide whether an exercise is good or bad, assess these 4 criteria:

1. **Stability**: does the exercise let you stay stable and isolate the target muscle without compensation?
2. **Progressive overload**: can you regularly increase the load with good execution?
3. **Movement path**: is the movement aligned with the actions of the targeted muscle?
4. **Resistance curve**: is the resistance well matched to the muscle's strength curve? (see skill `lifting-biomechanics`)

## Order of progression: frequency → volume → intensity

To organize this progression concretely within a structured program (macrocycle, mesocycles, microcycles), see skill `training-programming`.

Whatever the goal (hypertrophy, endurance, performance), progression must follow this strict order:

1. **Frequency** first: increase the number of sessions per week before anything else.
2. **Volume** next: increase the amount of work per session (sets, exercises, duration).
3. **Intensity** last: increase the load, the difficulty, the intensification techniques.

### Why this order?

- Increasing frequency lets the body **get used to regularity** and recover better.
- Increasing volume too early without enough frequency → insufficient recovery → stagnation/injury.
- Increasing intensity too early (heavy loads, advanced techniques, interval training) → risk of injury, burnout, and stagnating progress.
- This principle applies equally to strength training, cardio or any other sport.

### Application in strength training

- Start by training **regularly** (e.g. 3-4 stable sessions/week).
- Then gradually increase volume (number of sets per muscle group).
- Finally, introduce intensity (intensification techniques, drop sets, loads close to max).

## Intensification technique: isometric stretch at the end of a set

After reaching **concentric failure**, hold a **stretched** position (isometric) for 10-20 seconds while contracting the muscle as hard as possible.

### Principle

- On the last rep, instead of racking the load, stay in the bottom (stretched) position.
- **Push without moving** (maximal isometric contraction) → imagine lifting a ton while nothing moves.
- Keep the scapulae retracted, the body stable, no parasitic movement.

### Progressive application

| Level | Protocol |
|---|---|
| First time | 5-6 seconds on the last set only |
| Intermediate | 10-15 seconds on the last set |
| Advanced | 20 seconds on every set |

### Compatible exercises

Exercises with **high stretch tension** where the bottom position is safe:

- Dumbbell press (flat, incline, decline) → drop the dumbbells if needed.
- Overhead triceps extensions.
- Cable curl (stay in the stretched position).
- Stiff-leg deadlift (with good technique).
- Dumbbell flyes.

### Incompatible exercises

- **Squat, hack squat** → staying stuck at the bottom is dangerous and extremely traumatic in terms of fatigue/recovery.
- Any exercise where the stretched position puts the joints (shoulders, knees) at risk.

## Intensification technique: rest-pause

Rest-pause consists of reaching a **high total number of reps** (e.g. 50) in a single "set" interrupted by short pauses.

### Protocol

- Choose a moderate load (e.g. body weight ÷ 2 for the clean and press).
- Do as many reps as possible, stop for a few seconds, then resume, until the target total.
- The pace is dynamic, the pauses are short.
- Ideal at the **end of a session** for a maximal pump and a final volume stimulus.

## Intensification technique: clusters

Clusters consist of **splitting a set** into several groups of reps (clusters) with micro-recoveries of 5 to 15 seconds between each cluster.

### Protocol

- Instead of 1 × 12, do **4 × 3** or **3 × 4** with 10-15 seconds of rest between each cluster.
- This lets you use a **heavier** load than your usual max for the same total number of reps (e.g. 9 reps at 110 kg (240 lb) instead of 8 reps at 100 kg (220 lb)).
- More weight → more mechanical tension → potentially more muscle growth.

### When to use them

- Particularly suited to muscles with a majority of **fast-twitch fibers** (e.g. chest) which perform better on short efforts.
- Reserve for the **first 1-2 sets** of the main exercise, when you are freshest.
- Not on every set of every exercise → an occasional tool to optimize mechanical tension.

## Slow-twitch vs fast-twitch fibers: impact on recovery

Muscles with a majority of **slow-twitch fibers** (e.g. rear deltoid, calves, abs) tolerate large training volumes well and **don't need long recoveries** between sets (45-60 seconds is enough). They can also be trained at a higher frequency.

Muscles with a majority of **fast-twitch fibers** (e.g. chest) are very effective on short efforts but perform poorly on long sets. Performance drops quickly → favor short/medium sets and consider **clusters** to optimize the load.

## "Reminder" session: useless

The notion of a "reminder session" (a few light sets at the end of the week for a muscle already trained) is **ineffective**. Either do a **real**, complete session with progressive overload, or don't train at all. A reminder session doesn't provide enough stimulation to cause an adaptation.

## Muscle activation protocol (applicable to all muscles)

If a muscle is hard to feel/recruit (a common problem: lats, chest, rear deltoid…), a progressive activation protocol lets you (re)learn to contract it.

### 4-phase progression

1. **Unilateral isometric**: hold in the contracted position then in the stretched position (4 × 20 seconds per side). Find the exact position where you feel the contraction.
2. **Unilateral dynamic**: eccentric + concentric movement with a very light load, keeping the contraction from start to finish (4 × 20 reps per side).
3. **Bilateral dynamic**: same thing but with both arms (harder to manage both sides at once).
4. **Back to unilateral if needed**: if going bilateral loses the contraction (common in the stretched position), return to unilateral and progress.

### Duration and frequency

- 30-40 minutes per activation session.
- **1 to 3 sessions** are generally enough to master the recruitment.
- Once the motor pattern is acquired, it is permanent → return to classic training.

### Recommended tools

- **Resistance band** for the isometric steps (gentle resistance, easy to adjust).
- **Cable** for the dynamic steps (constant tension, adjustable load).
- Placing the **free hand** on the targeted muscle helps confirm activation (tactile feedback).

Choose the tools from `profile.equipment` (or equipment-free).

## Importance of the eccentric: don't release the contraction

A muscle movement includes a **concentric** phase (shortening) AND an **eccentric** phase (lengthening under tension). Doing only the concentric = doing only **50% of the work**.

- Maintain the muscle contraction from start to finish of every rep, including during the eccentric.
- Classic mistake: contracting only when pulling/pushing, then **completely relaxing** on the return phase.
- This problem is often unconscious — the lifter doesn't realize they are not contracting on the eccentric.
- Link with tempo: the controlled eccentric (~3 seconds, see skill `lifting-biomechanics`) naturally forces you to keep the contraction.

## Execution stability: quality criterion of a set

The **first rep and the last rep** of a set must look visually identical. If you filmed the set, you shouldn't be able to tell which rep is the first and which is the last.

### Signs of lost stability

- The **head** moves back and forth (frequent compensation when fatigue sets in).
- The **scapulae** loosen (loss of scapular retraction).
- The **glutes** lift off the bench (involuntary leg drive).
- The lever arm shrinks (the dumbbells move closer together = involuntary mechanical drop set).

### Recommendation

- Reduce the load or the range of motion rather than lose stability.
- Stability is the **first criterion** to preserve, before the number of reps or the load.

## Hypertrophy: multiple valid methods

Building muscle is possible with very varied approaches:

- Short or long sets.
- Short or long recoveries.
- 3 or 6 training days per week.
- Fast or slow eccentrics.

The general recommendations (controlled eccentric, good execution, controlled tempo) are aimed at the **greatest number**. An advanced lifter (5+ years) can diversify their methods according to their preferences, as long as the fundamentals (progressive overload, correct execution, recovery) stay in place.

## Combining cardio and strength training

### What the studies say

- **Older studies** (1980, 2012): suggested interference between cardio and strength development. These results fed the popular belief that cardio "kills muscle gains".
- **Recent meta-analyses** (2022, 43 studies + 25 studies): combining endurance + strength training **compromises neither hypertrophy nor maximal strength development**, under certain conditions.
- **Health impact** (2019 meta-analysis, 11 studies): strength training alone → -21% mortality risk. Cardio + strength training → **-40% mortality risk**.

### Conditions to avoid interference

1. **Never do cardio and strength training in the same session.** Run a 10 km then follow with a leg session → zero performance. Same for interval training before an upper-body session.
2. **Separate the two workouts by at least 5-6 hours** (e.g. cardio at 9am, strength training at 4pm).
3. **Integrate cardio gradually**: start with a small easy run at base endurance pace (zone 2, nasal breathing) on recovery days. E.g. 5 km → 8 km after a month → 2 sessions of 5 km/week.
4. **Respect the ratio**: never do more cardio volume than strength-training volume. Recommended ratio: **2/3 strength training, 1/3 cardio**.

### Benefits of cardio for strength training

- Improved **VO2 max** and cardiovascular fitness.
- Clearly improved **recovery capacity** → strength sessions become less tiring at equal volume.
- No muscle mass loss observed (including over 2 years of combined practice).

### Special cases

- **Competition preparation** (bodybuilding or endurance): the competition activity takes priority, the other goes to the minimum.
- **Hybrid athletes** (sub-3h marathon + 230 kg (505 lb) squat): these are **exceptions**, not the norm. Don't compare yourself to them.
- A lifter who has already reached their muscular potential risks less by adding cardio than a beginner who still has a lot of gains to make → hence the importance of gradual progression.

When planning cardio, only use cardio equipment listed in `profile.equipment`.

## Patience and commitment to your program

Progress is a **slow** process measured in months and years, not weeks.

### Principles

- Don't skip steps at the risk of stagnating, regressing or getting injured.
- Commit to a training program and **follow it from start to finish**, even if it isn't perfect.
- If the program didn't work → correct and modify it. But don't change midway without reason.
- The patience developed in strength training (19 years of experience, per the source) is transferable to any other sport.

### Avoid outside noise

- **Don't compare yourself** to other lifters, especially via social media.
- Constant exposure to others' performances gives the impression of doing the wrong things → weathervane effect (constantly changing programs) → no progress.
- **Recommendation**: focus only on your own program, mute sources of comparison, and only share your results once the goal is reached.

## Supplementation: creatine

Creatine is the most studied and most validated dietary supplement in sports nutrition (70,000 publications on PubMed). It is a key tool for strength-training performance.

### Mechanism

- Creatine is stored in metabolically active tissues: **95% in the muscles**, ~5% in the brain.
- In the muscle, it is converted to **phosphocreatine** → can donate phosphate to regenerate ATP (fast energy).
- Result: a few extra seconds of explosive energy → lift heavier, more reps, more volume → better progress.

### Standard dose: 3-5 g/day

- More than enough to **saturate muscle stores** and improve athletic performance.
- Recommended form: **creatine monohydrate** (the most studied).
- No need for a loading phase (20 g/day for 5 days) unless time-constrained (imminent competition). In regular strength-training practice, 3-5 g/day long term produces the same results.

### Maxi-dosing: ~30 g/day (0.35 g/kg of body weight)

Recent studies (2023-2024) show that at high doses, creatine goes beyond simple muscle saturation and **accumulates in the brain**:

- Improved **short-term memory** and reasoning abilities.
- Can **offset the effects of a short night** of sleep or fatigue on cognitive performance.
- **Neuroprotective** role (oxidative stress, degeneration).
- Particularly interesting for: older people, stressed individuals, vegetarians (no dietary creatine).

### Safety

- Creatine is one of the **safest** supplements there is.
- No kidney damage, not a steroid, does not increase body fat.
- Can be used in small dose (3-5 g) or high dose (~30 g) with no established risk.

### Practical use

| Situation | Recommended dose |
|---|---|
| Daily strength training | 3-5 g/day (standard) |
| Imminent competition (loading phase) | 20 g/day for 5 days, then 3-5 g/day |
| Fatigue, lack of sleep, cognitive need | ~30 g as a one-off dose (0.35 g/kg) |

### Dietary sources

- Red meat and fish are rich in creatine, but you would need ~1 kg of red meat to get just 1 g of creatine.
- The body makes its own creatine (liver, kidneys, pancreas) from glycine, methionine and arginine, and adjusts production according to dietary intake.

## Nutrition: calories and nutrition labels

### Energy value of macronutrients

The reference caloric values (Atwater factors, late 19th century):

| Macronutrient | Calories per gram |
|---|---|
| Protein | ~4 kcal (actually ~3.8-3.9) |
| Carbohydrates | ~4 kcal |
| Fat | ~9 kcal |
| Alcohol | ~7 kcal |

The caloric value of a food = sum of the energies of its macronutrients. Note: protein×4 + carbohydrates×4 + fat×9 does not always give exactly the displayed calorie total — this is normal and inherent to the calculation methods.

### Reading a nutrition label (Europe)

In the EU (Regulation 1169/2011), the **mandatory** items on any food product are:

- Energy value (in kJ **and** kcal)
- Fat
- Saturated fatty acids
- Carbohydrates
- Of which sugars
- Protein
- Salt

### Common pitfalls

- **Manipulated portions**: manufacturers can define ridiculously small portions to display low values. Always refer to the **per 100 g** column (mandatory in Europe, but not in the United States).
- **Marketing claims**: "0% fat", "supports your metabolism", "-25% salt" — these claims don't appear in the nutrition table and are **marketing**, not nutrition. Don't rely on them to judge a food's quality.
- **Rounding and uncertainties**: every analysis has a measurement uncertainty (10 to 50% depending on the instrument and the parameter measured). For a food showing 12 g of protein, the real value may be between 11.5 and 12.5 g. The more processed/prepared a food, the larger the potential margin of error.

### Label reliability

Laboratory tests on common foods (chips, basmati rice, skyr, ready meals) show that labels are **generally reliable** — the gaps stay within the announced uncertainty margins. Two methods exist to determine the values:

1. **Database** (e.g. CIQUAL): cheaper, less precise.
2. **Laboratory analysis**: technical instruments measuring each nutrient precisely — much more precise.

### Counting calories: recommended approach

- **Consistent method**: always weigh the same way (all raw or all cooked), never mix. What matters is the consistency of the method, not the choice of raw vs cooked. Cooking doesn't make calories disappear.
- **A temporary tool, not a prison**: calorie counting is useful during a learning period to get familiar with the energy value of foods. The goal is to gradually detach from it and eat intuitively in an informed way.
- **Don't reduce eating to calories**: food is nutrients (micro and macro), balance, and pleasure — not just fuel or bricks for muscle mass.
