# Obliques training

## Anatomy

### External oblique

| Characteristic | Detail |
|---|---|
| Origin | Ribs (interdigitating with the serratus anterior) |
| Insertion | Pelvis (pubis), opposite side |
| Fiber path | **Crossed diagonal**: from the rib cage on one side toward the pubis on the other side |
| Unilateral action | **Rotation** of the torso toward the opposite side + lateral flexion |
| Bilateral action | Spinal flexion + anterior pelvic tilt (actions shared with the rectus abdominis) |
| Respiratory role | **Expiratory** → contracts when you exhale |

The serrated look visible in people with low body fat is actually the **junction** between the external oblique and the serratus anterior, not the oblique muscle itself.

### Internal oblique

| Characteristic | Detail |
|---|---|
| Origin | Iliac crest of the pelvis |
| Insertion | Ribs (same side) |
| Fiber path | **Nearly vertical**: stays on the same side (no crossing) |
| Unilateral action | Lateral **tilt** (rib cage moves toward the iliac crest on the same side) |
| Bilateral action | Spinal flexion + anterior pelvic tilt |

### Shared actions

The rectus abdominis, external oblique and internal oblique share the actions of **spinal flexion** and **expiration**. Exhaling and drawing in the belly during ab exercises lets you contract all three muscles + the transversus abdominis simultaneously.

## Key principle: the obliques respond very well to isometrics

The obliques (and the abdominal wall in general) respond particularly well to **isometric** exercises: anti-rotation, anti-lateral-flexion, active core bracing. This is explained by their everyday role of stabilizing the trunk.

## Exercise selection (3 exercises are enough)

### Exercise 1: Pallof press (anti-rotation)

Isometric movement in which you resist a rotation produced by a cable or a resistance band.

- **Position**: side-on to the cable/band, cable at chest height.
- **Movement**: alternate between arms flexed (hands against the chest) and arms extended in front of you. Arms extended = maximal lever arm → maximal resistance to rotation.
- **Main target**: **external** oblique.
- **Variations**: standing, kneeling (more stable).
- Can be done with a **resistance band** (handy for warm-ups or outdoors).
- The cable is preferable for progressive overload.

#### Variation: suitcase deadlift (anti-lateral-flexion)

Deadlift with **a single dumbbell** on one side → resists lateral flexion.

- **Main target**: **internal** oblique.
- Compound movement → good calorie expenditure + abdominal wall work.
- Allows significant progressive overload (sets with 40-45 kg possible for advanced lifters).
- Good exercise for learning **abdominal breathing** under load.

### Exercise 2: Hanging knee raises with a twist

Hanging from a pull-up bar, perform a spinal flexion then **tilt the knees** to one side in the top position.

- **Target**: external oblique + internal oblique + rectus abdominis.
- **Execution**: start the flexion on the midline, then at mid-range, direct the knees toward one side. Do all the reps on one side before switching to the other (don't alternate left-right every rep → too much inertia, lower contraction quality).
- **Beginner variation**: same movement on a bench inclined at 30-45°. The more upright the bench, the harder it is.
- The hanging version is **more effective** (more mechanical tension on the abdominal wall).

### Exercise 3: Sprints (15 seconds)

Sprints cause a **powerful** isometric contraction of the obliques to resist the rotations generated by arm movements at maximum speed.

- The most intense oblique soreness often follows interval sessions, not classic ab exercises.
- **Double effect**: muscle development of the obliques + high calorie expenditure → helps reduce body fat → visible obliques.

#### Sprint protocol

- **Warm-up**: 8-10 minutes with progressive accelerations at 70-80% of max.
- **Session**: 10 sprints of 15 seconds at **absolute maximum speed** (100%).
- **Recovery**: free between each sprint — take as long as needed to be able to start again at 100%. If you can't reach max speed, recovery is insufficient.
- **Progression**: beginners → start with 3-4 sprints, increase gradually.

#### Why 15 seconds and not longer?

Beyond 15 seconds (30s, 1 min...), you can no longer maintain maximum speed → less force generated → less oblique contraction. Intensity takes priority over duration.

## Body fat level: the condition for visibility

Developing the obliques is not enough to make them visible. Visibility depends directly on **body fat level**. A suitable nutrition plan (see the `training-principles` skill → "Nutrition: fat loss") is essential to reveal the obliques.

## Obliques program

### Dedicated session (cardio + obliques, 1-2x/week)

| # | Exercise | Sets | Reps | Rest | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Pallof press | 6 (3L + 3R) | 10 | Short (alternating L/R) | Tempo 2-0-2-0. Advanced: hold 2s with arms extended |
| 1bis | *OR* Suitcase deadlift | 6 (3L + 3R) | 10 | A bit longer | Compound, more tiring |
| 2 | Hanging knee raises + twist | 4 (2L + 2R) | Max (to failure) | 30-45 seconds | All reps on one side before switching |
| 3 | Sprints | 10 | 15 seconds | Free (full recovery) | 8-10 min warm-up mandatory |

### If added at the end of a strength session (no sprints)

| # | Exercise | Sets | Reps | Rest |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Pallof press | 6 (3L + 3R) | 10 | Short |
| 2 | Hanging knee raises + twist | 4 (2L + 2R) | Max | 30-45 seconds |

### Placement in the week

- **Dedicated session**: don't place sprints after a heavy leg or push session → impossible to reach 100% of your capacity.
- Maximum frequency: **2 times per week** (sprinting is very aggressive for the muscular system).
- If no dedicated session → the first 2 exercises at the end of a strength session + sprints in another session.
