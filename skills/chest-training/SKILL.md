# Chest training

## Anatomy of the pectoralis major

The pectoralis major is made up of fibers oriented in different directions:

| Portion | Origin | Fiber direction | Specific action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clavicular head | Clavicle | Fibers running downward to the humerus | Adduction + arm elevation |
| Sternal head | Sternum | Horizontal fibers | Pure adduction |
| Costal (abdominal) head | Ribs | Fibers running upward | Adduction + arm depression |

The pectoralis major is **single-joint** (it only crosses the shoulder joint). Its fan-shaped fibers explain why different working angles target different portions. Internal rotation is a secondary action that is not relevant to strength-training exercises.

### Predominantly fast-twitch fibers

The pectoralis major has a **high proportion of fast-twitch fibers**. Consequences:

- Very effective for short efforts, performance drops off quickly on long sets.
- Favor short to medium sets (6-12 reps).
- **Clusters** are particularly well suited to the chest (see the `training-principles` skill → clusters).

### Impact of morphology

An exercise's effectiveness for the chest depends heavily on individual morphology (see the `lifting-biomechanics` skill):

- **Thick rib cage + inclined sternum** → more fibers opposing gravity on the incline press → potentially effective exercise.
- **Flat rib cage + vertical sternum** → very few clavicular fibers loaded on the incline → less relevant exercise, the anterior deltoid takes over.

## Exercise ranking (worst to best)

### Avoid

#### Squeeze press (dumbbells)
- The hope is to contract the pecs by squeezing the dumbbells against each other, but with free weights the force is **vertical** (gravity) and you are trying to apply a **horizontal** force → no biomechanical sense.
- The burning sensation "inside the pecs" does not guarantee real stimulation.
- Throw it in the trash.

#### Cable fly facing the pulleys (standing, stepping forward)
- Very poor **stability**: you step forward several paces, the weight pulls you backward → other muscles are forced to compensate.
- Mediocre **resistance curve**: closed angle at the end of the movement (minimal tension), too much tension at the start.
- Painful **setup**: you take up a disproportionate amount of working space in the gym.
- You might as well do flat or incline flyes, or better: the seated version.

#### 45° incline press (barbell, dumbbells, guided)
- Ultra-popular but overrated for the majority of morphologies.
- At 45°, the fibers directly opposing gravity are those of the **anterior deltoid**, not the clavicular fibers of the pectoral.
- 2010 EMG study: raising the incline beyond 30° does not improve clavicular activation and recruits the anterior deltoid more.
- The flatter the rib cage, the less relevant the exercise is for the pecs.
- Can be replaced by an inclined converging machine press or a slightly decline press.

### Good exercise

#### Cable fly facing the pulleys (seated, stabilized)
- Same exercise as the standing version but with a **seat** → much better stability.
- Body positioned **between the two pulleys** → better resistance curve (hard at the end = high contraction tension, but also hard at the start).
- **Single-joint** exercise → little nervous fatigue.
- **Adaptable**: change the hand position (upward = upper pecs, downward = lower pecs) to target virtually all the fibers.
- **Execution**: shoulder blades squeezed together from start to finish, elbows locked (single-joint movement).
- The pec deck (butterfly) is a decent alternative but the cable is preferable (more flexibility, no fixed axes of rotation).

### Very good exercise

#### Converging machine press
- The machine's convergence is a major asset (see the `lifting-biomechanics` skill → converging machines).
- True **constant tension** thanks to the machine's architecture.
- Well-suited resistance curve: harder at the top than at the bottom → reduces anterior deltoid involvement.
- Works for most morphologies.
- Available in an incline or decline version.
- Quality varies by machine brand.

### Best exercises (tied)

#### Dips (parallel bars)
- The slight natural **decline** of dips puts the pectoral fibers in an excellent position relative to the line of force.
- Potentially loads **all the fibers** of the pecs (including clavicular), depending on morphology (rib cage thickness, sternum inclination).
- Golden-age bodybuilders (Schwarzenegger, Lou Ferrigno, Serge Nubret) credited dips for a large part of their chest development.
- If there were **only one exercise** for the pecs → weighted dips.
- **Caveat**: some lifters may have sternum or shoulder pain → adapt the range of motion and increase the load gradually.

##### Progression toward dips

| Step | Condition | Action |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Can't do 30 push-ups in a row | Work on push-ups, no dips |
| 2 | 30 push-ups OK, dips painful | Slightly decline press, work on mobility |
| 3 | Bodyweight dips without pain | Increase range of motion gradually |
| 4 | Sufficient range of motion and strength | Weighted dips, progressive overload |

#### Slightly decline press
- Same logic as dips: the decline puts more pectoral fibers in direct opposition to gravity.
- Can potentially load all the fibers, including the clavicular ones.
- Ideally, pair it with an inclined converging machine press or flyes for complete work.
- If only one movement for the pecs → dips or slightly decline press.

## Exercises not ranked above but valid

These exercises are good and can be used, but are not at the top of the ranking:

- **Bench press**: good exercise but can be risky depending on morphology.
- **Push-ups**: improve stability, work the pectorals and above all the **serratus anterior** (by protracting the shoulder blades in the top position). Ideal at the end of a session for shoulder health.
- **Fly press**: hybrid press/fly movement that modifies the resistance curve (easier at the bottom, harder at the top).

## Cable crossover (clavicular head)

- **Contraction tension** exercise, ideal for finishing a chest session and getting a maximal pump.
- **Pulley setup**: align the cable + the humerus with the direction of the **clavicular fibers** (path sloping slightly downward and forward).
- Pronated grip is fine.
- **Smooth** movement, no jerking: no two-part movement.
- Good choice when the pecs are a strong point and you want to finish with contracted work.

## Clavicular head focus: specific exercises

If the upper chest is a weak point, general exercises are not enough. You need a dedicated session with movements whose path is **aligned with the clavicular fibers**.

### What the science says

- **2010 EMG study**: raising the bench incline has very little effect on activation of the clavicular head. Beyond 15-30°, the anterior deltoid takes over.
- **2020 EMG study**: an incline of **30°** gives the best activation of the clavicular head. Beyond that, the anterior deltoid gets too involved.
- **Conclusion**: avoid inclines > 30°. Favor the **semi-incline (~30°)** to target the clavicular head.

### Exercise 1: Unilateral cable press (fly press)

Hybrid press/fly movement: start like a press, finish like a fly.

- **Modified resistance curve**: easier at the bottom (where the anterior deltoid compensates) and harder at the top → reduces compensation.
- **Pulley setup**: between the chest and the navel. Adjust according to clavicle width (narrow clavicles → lower pulley so the movement goes more upward).
- Unilateral: the free hand grabs something for stability.
- **Hand grip**: test supination, neutral and pronation. Supination often helps you feel the upper pecs better.
- Controlled movement, technique is paramount, no cheating.

### Exercise 2: Guillotine press (guided machine)

Variation created by **Vince Gironda** (Arnold Schwarzenegger's coach).

- Flat press with the bar at **clavicle** level (almost at the neck).
- **Elbows raised** as high as possible, shoulder blades squeezed together, shoulders as low as possible.
- **Half range of motion** mandatory: stop when the humerus is parallel to the floor. Don't bring the bar close to the neck.
- **No heavy loads**: this is a feel and learning exercise for clavicular contraction.
- Do it on a guided machine for stability and a vertical path.
- Beginners: start with the empty bar for long sets.

### Exercise 3: Semi-incline dumbbell press (~30°)

- Incline at **~30°** (the optimal angle according to the 2020 EMG study).
- Arm/torso angle rather **closed** (elbows not flared too much) to align the movement with the clavicular fibers.
- Allows **heavy loads** (almost as much as the flat press) → high mechanical tension.
- This is the main exercise for progressive overload on the clavicular head.

### Exercise 4: Push-ups (serratus anterior + overall stimulation)

- At the end of the session, full range of motion.
- **Squeeze the shoulder blades together** in the bottom position, **protract the shoulder blades** in the top position.
- The protraction at the top contracts the **serratus anterior** → crucial for shoulder health.
- Works stability + overall stimulation (sternal + costal) to complement the clavicular exercises.

## Clavicular head focus session

| # | Exercise | Sets × Reps | Tempo | Rest | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Unilateral cable press (fly press) | 4 × 12 per arm | 2-0-1-1 | 45s between each arm | Pause 1s at the contraction. Test supination |
| 2 | Guillotine press (guided machine) | 4 × 10 | 2-0-2-0 | 90s | Half range of motion, moderate loads |
| 3 | Semi-incline dumbbell press (~30°) | 5 × 6 | 1-0-X-0 | Free | Heavy load, explosive concentric |
| 4 | Push-ups | 1 × 50 (rest-pause) | Free | 15-20s between sets | Full range of motion, protract shoulder blades at the top |

### Recommended frequency

- Ideal: **3 times every 15 days**.
- 2 times per week: possible but may be a touch too much volume.
- Placement: at the start of a push session (PPL) or in a dedicated chest/back session.

## Execution tips for the chest

### Shoulder blades squeezed together from start to finish

- This is rule #1 on all chest movements (presses, flyes, dips).
- It is better to **reduce the range of motion** than to let the scapulae spread apart.
- **Do not fully lock out the arms** at the top of presses: the last few centimeters provide very little additional stimulation (especially on a converging machine where resistance decreases at the end of the stroke).

#### Scapular calibration exercise

To determine your maximum top range of motion on presses:

1. With your arms spread out to each side of the body, **squeeze the scapulae together as hard as you can** (shoulders as low as possible).
2. Slowly bring both arms together in front of you.
3. **Stop** as soon as the scapulae start to spread apart.
4. This point = your **maximum top range of motion** on presses.

Film yourself from behind (in a fitted t-shirt) to check that the scapulae don't move.

### Recommended range of motion

| Movement | Top range | Bottom range |
|---|---|---|
| Presses (machine, dumbbells) | ~75% (don't lock out) | Maximum (full stretch) |
| Dumbbell flyes | Only ~60% | Maximum (very strong stretch) |
| Cable crossover | Full contraction | Depending on mobility |

### Flyes: arm position

- **Do not** open the arms to 90° relative to the trunk. Keep a slightly **closed** angle (elbows a bit lower).
- This keeps the shoulders as **low** as possible and protects the joint.
- On a semi-incline or incline, lower the angle even more → same path as the cable crossover to target the clavicular head.
- Elbows **locked** (single-joint movement).

### Flat flyes: can target all the fibers

The exercise does **not automatically** dictate which fibers are loaded — it is the **execution** that decides. Flat flyes can target the clavicular, sternal or costal fibers by changing the path of the arms. Don't be a prisoner of the "exercise = zone" association (e.g. incline flyes = upper pecs). Test different paths on the same exercise.

### Head still

- The head must **not** move back and forth during the reps. It is a sign of compensation and loss of scapular stability (see the `training-principles` skill → execution stability).

### Isometric stretch at the end of a set

- Applicable to all dumbbell chest movements and the converging machine.
- 10-20 seconds in the bottom position, maximal isometric contraction.
- On dumbbell flyes: you can drop the dumbbells if necessary → relatively safe.
- See the `training-principles` skill → intensification techniques.

## Recommended volume

- **Per session**: 2-3 exercises, 3-5 sets each = **9-12 sets** max (25 sets is ridiculous, even for a weak point).
- **Frequency**: 2-3 times per week depending on the format (PPL, split, hybrid).
- Cover both types of tension (stretch + contraction) and the different zones (clavicular, sternal, costal).
- Avoid **redundant** exercises in the same session (e.g. barbell bench + dumbbell bench + machine bench = 3× the same stretch tension on the same fibers → useless volume).
- Short rest times (~1 min 30 s-2 min) combined with a controlled tempo → high pump and stimulation even with moderate loads.
- See `training-principles`, "Weekly volume per muscle".

## Minimal chest program

| Exercise | Type | Sets |
|---|---|---|
| Dips or slightly decline press | Compound, all fibers | 3 |
| Converging machine press | Constant tension, good resistance curve | 3 |
| Cable fly facing the pulleys (seated) or dumbbell flyes | Single-joint, stretch | 3 |
| Cable crossover (clavicular) | Contraction tension, pump | 3 |

| # | Exercise | Sets | Technique |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Decline converging machine press | 3-4 | 3s eccentric tempo, 75% top range, 20s isometric stretch on the last set |
