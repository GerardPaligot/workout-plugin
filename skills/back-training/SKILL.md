# Back training

## Anatomy: the back ≠ the lats

The "back" groups together several muscles with different actions. An effective program must distinguish between them.

### Back muscles

| Muscle | Visual role | Category |
|---|---|---|
| Latissimus dorsi (lats) | Back width | Width |
| Teres major | Back width (under the armpit) | Width |
| Trapezius (upper/middle/lower) | Back thickness | Thickness |
| Erector spinae | Back thickness | Thickness |
| Quadratus lumborum (lower back) | Back thickness | Thickness |
| Posterior deltoid | Back thickness | Thickness |
| Teres minor | — | — |

### Wide back vs thick back

- **Wide back** = development of the latissimus dorsi + teres major.
- **Thick back** = development of the trapezius + erector spinae + quadratus lumborum + posterior deltoid.
- For both → rigorous organization of the program.

## Latissimus dorsi vs teres major: a recruitment problem

The latissimus dorsi is one of the muscles that is **hardest to feel and recruit**. Most lifters develop a powerful teres major without ever really activating the lats.

### Why it is hard

- The teres major, smaller and more superficial, tends to **compensate** systematically on pulling movements.
- The more the teres major is worked (even unintentionally), the stronger it gets → the more it is **favored** in future movements → vicious circle.
- Result: years of lifting with a developed teres major and underdeveloped lats.
- Well-developed lats give the famous "wings" (back width seen from the front), which the teres major alone cannot produce.

### How to tell the two apart during contraction

- The **teres major** contracts just under the armpit (upper and outer part of the back).
- The **latissimus dorsi** contracts lower and wider (lateral "wings" of the torso).
- Placing a hand on the targeted area helps confirm which muscle is actually activated.

## Lat activation protocol (6 steps)

This progressive protocol teaches (or re-teaches) you to recruit the lats specifically. It follows a logic of increasing complexity: isometric → dynamic, unilateral → bilateral, contraction → stretch.

### Duration and frequency

- 30-40 minutes per session.
- In general, **1 to 3 sessions** are enough to master lat recruitment.
- Once acquired, the motor pattern is permanent → return to regular training.

### Step 1 — Isometric in contraction (resistance band, unilateral)

- Set a resistance band at **elbow height**.
- Pull the elbow slightly backward, hand relaxed in the band.
- Adjust the torso position (slight inward/outward rotation) to find the **best lat contraction**.
- **Crunch** the abs very slightly to bring the elbow closer to the iliac crest.
- Lean slightly **to the side** to maximize the elbow ↔ iliac crest approach.
- **4 × 20-second holds** per side.

### Step 2 — Isometric in stretch (band set high, unilateral)

- Place the band **as high as possible** (pull-up bar, lat pulldown).
- Same principle: hand in the band, pull very slightly.
- The elbow must point **inward** → alignment with the lat fibers, which converge toward the iliac crest.
- Don't crunch too much to the side → otherwise the obliques take over.
- **4 × 20-second holds** per side.

### Step 3 — Dynamic in contraction (cable at elbow height, unilateral)

- Set the cable at **elbow height**.
- Find the position imprinted in step 1.
- Perform an eccentric (slow) and a concentric movement while maintaining **lat contraction from start to finish**.
- Very light load (just enough to feel it).
- **4 × 20 reps** per side.

### Step 4 — Dynamic in stretch (high cable, unilateral)

- Cable in the high position.
- Come down, find the contraction, then dynamic movement.
- **Stay contracted in all phases** of the movement (concentric AND eccentric).
- Place the free hand on the lat to confirm activation (tactile feedback).
- **4 × 20 reps** per side.

### Step 5 — Bilateral on the lat pulldown

- Shoulder-width grip.
- Starting position: **shoulder blades forward** (NO scapular retraction).
- The elbows point slightly **down and inward**, aiming at the iliac crest.
- Slight back crunch, knees slightly bent.
- **No arching**, no squeezed shoulder blades → both of these mistakes disengage the lats.
- "200 kg" trick: in the bottom position, imagine the cable is pulling 200 kg and that you have to resist → intentional isometric contraction → maximal lat activation.
- **4 × 20 reps** (start with a half range of motion if necessary).

### Step 6 — Unilateral on the lat pulldown (if step 5 is too hard)

- If the contraction is lost in the bilateral version in the stretch, go back to **unilateral** on the lat pulldown.
- Keep the **forearm perpendicular to the floor** → long lever arm at the shoulder → more lat work.
- Handle **slightly away from the shoulder** (arm not too flexed) → increases the lever arm.
- Progress to the full range of motion once the contraction is mastered at half range.
- **4 × 20 reps** per side.

### Common mistakes on the lat pulldown

| Mistake | Consequence |
|---|---|
| Shoulder blades squeezed in retraction | Works the trapezius, not the lats |
| Excessive arching | Shifts the work to the erector spinae |
| Elbows too far out | Misalignment with the fibers → teres major takes over |
| Handle very close to the shoulder | Short lever arm → less lat stimulus |
| Letting go of the eccentric | Loss of 50% of the muscle work |

## Exercises for the lats: 2 are enough

The action of the latissimus dorsi is simple:
- **Retropulsion**: bringing the arm backward.
- **Adduction**: bringing the arm inward.

### Minimal effective programming

| Type | Tension | Example | Sets |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vertical pull | High stretch tension | Lat pulldown (high cable) | 3-4 |
| Horizontal pull | High contraction tension | Seated row / One-arm dumbbell row | 3-4 |

With these 2 exercises (3-4 sets each), the lats are worked through the full range of motion. Doing 4-5 exercises for the lats = wasted time + negative impact on recovery.

## Lat exercise ranking (worst to best)

### Avoid

#### T-bar row
- The handles are **too close together** → forces you to flare the elbows at the end of the movement to complete the range of motion.
- The more the elbows flare, the **less the lats work**.
- Works the trapezius and erector spinae slightly, but to target the lats it is the worst choice.
- Encourages ego lifting (lots of plates on the bar) → heavy load that hurts recovery without stimulating the lats.
- **Alternative**: do the movement unilaterally, leaning slightly to the side, to align the path with the fibers.

#### High-cable pullover (bent-forward version)
- When you lean forward 45-90°, the resistance curve is **totally unbalanced**: very hard at the start (lat deeply stretched = weak), very easy at the end (see the `lifting-biomechanics` skill).
- The stretched lat cannot produce enough force → triceps compensate over the first few centimeters.
- **Corrected version (good exercise)**: stay more **upright** → more balanced resistance curve, less compensation.
- **Optimal version**: unilateral, leaning slightly to the side → perfect path, turns a mediocre exercise into an exceptional one.

#### One-arm dumbbell row (single-arm row)
- High contraction tension exercise, but often executed very poorly.
- Much more interesting variations exist in terms of path, resistance curve, stability and progressive overload.
- Remains valid if you have no cable/machine (home training, crowded gym).
- **Correct execution if used**: elbow close to the body, vertical path, aim the dumbbell at the iliac crest, play with the rotation of the upper body.
- Can be relevant for the trapezius (different execution, elbow flared).
- **Disclaimer**: can be a good exercise for the trapezius (upper/middle) with an adapted execution.

#### Barbell bent-over row (incl. Yates row)
- Source's view (he says the debate remains open): not the most relevant exercise for the lats; horizontal pulls and lat-pulldown-type pulls are more coherent for them.
- Rows stay very effective for trapezius, upper back and rear delts; the source prefers other exercises for those.
- **Alternative**: unilateral cable row, small rotation of the upper body → very intense lat contraction. Or a unilateral divergent machine (Hammer Strength) where torso position can be adjusted (see "Unilateral work" below).
- Trade-off accepted by the source: loses erector (and possibly trapezius) work.

### Good exercise

#### Supinated-grip lat pulldown
- Performs one of the lats' main actions: **retropulsion** (bringing the arm backward).
- Deeply stretched position → high stretch tension → very interesting for hypertrophy.
- Excellent muscle range of motion if well executed.
- Favorite exercise of Dorian Yates (6× Mr. Olympia, nicknamed "The Shadow" for the width of his back).
- **Risk**: same problem as the bent-forward pullover → very stretched start of the movement → possible compensation.
- **Tips**: (1) don't start with the arm straight but slightly bent, (2) lean back very slightly → lat less stretched at the start → better recruitment.
- As you progress, you can increase the range of motion gradually.
- **Neutral grip**: almost equivalent to supination. Slight preference for supination because it loads the forearms less (varies from person to person).
- **Pronated grip**: not at all the same thing (works adduction more than retropulsion).

### Best exercises

#### Pronated-grip pull-ups — 2nd place
- Work the second main action of the lats: **adduction** (bringing the arm inward).
- Perfect complement to the supinated-grip lat pulldown (retropulsion).
- Bodyweight exercise → requires mastering your own physique (like dips, push-ups).
- **Limitations**: hard to master, potential lack of stability, high body fat = problematic, complex progressive overload (adding a weight belt, chain).
- **Advice**: place in a cycle with short sets, or use added weight for the more advanced.
- Very close to 1st place.

#### Cable lat pull (unilateral, seated) — 1st place
- **The best exercise** for the lats. Ticks absolutely every box:
  - **Path**: mix of retropulsion and adduction → perfectly aligned with the lats' actions.
  - **Resistance curve**: perfectly balanced (not too hard at the start, not too easy at the end) → fibers able to produce maximum force without compensation.
  - **Stability**: seated on a small seat → maximal external stability + abs contraction for internal stability.
  - **Progressive overload**: easy on the cable (gradual increase of the load).
  - **Unilateral**: lets you align the path with the muscle fibers.
  - **Infinite customization**: pronated/neutral/supinated grip depending on the individual, adjustment of body position.
- You could virtually do only this exercise and get exceptional results for the lats.
- Seated stability → muscle fibers produce even more force → better recruitment.

## Trapezius work (source's view)
- Horizontal pull for the traps / upper back, elbows well out, ~4 × 12: machine, low cable, or bent-over barbell/dumbbell row (this video lists it as an option). Reinforces the one-arm dumbbell row note (elbow flared for the trapezius).
- Shrug 3 × 15: not his favourite, but useful for the upper trapezius; responds to longer rep ranges.

## Unilateral work: superior for the back

Unilateral work (one arm at a time) is recommended for most back exercises.

### Why?

- The lat fibers run from the outside to the inside.
- A **converging** movement (outside → inside) is necessary to stimulate them properly.
- Bilaterally, the movement is linear → you never reach the full stretch or the full contraction.
- Unilaterally, you can adjust body position to align the movement path with the muscle fibers.

### Exercises concerned

- Vertical pulls (whatever the grip)
- Horizontal pulls (whatever the grip)
- Straight-arm pulldowns

**Exception**: pull-ups are done bilaterally.

## Pull-ups: nearly indispensable

Pull-ups are a fundamental movement for the back (not just the lats).

### Muscles worked

- Latissimus dorsi
- Teres major
- Elbow flexors (biceps, forearms)
- Middle and lower trapezius

### Key points

- Hypertrophy-mode execution: vertical path, no compensation with the body.
- Difficulty increased if bodyweight or body fat is too high.
- Movement very often poorly executed in the gym (ego + compensation).
- Comparison: pull-ups are to the back what dips are to the chest.
- Complex progressive overload → place in a cycle with short sets or use added weight.
- Even with 15 years of experience, it is hard to string together more than 15 reps without degrading execution.

## Hand grip: nuances depending on the pull

Grips are not strictly equivalent depending on the exercise:

- **Lat pulldown**: supinated and neutral work **retropulsion**, pronated works **adduction**. These are different actions of the lats → ideally to be combined.
- **Supinated vs neutral**: nearly equivalent, slight preference for supination (less forearm involvement, varies from person to person).
- For overall lat development, the grip itself is **not the determining factor** → choose the one that gives the best feel and the best progressive overload.

## The deadlift is NOT a lat exercise

- It is an exercise for the **posterior chain** (calves → trapezius).
- It does not let you isolate a back muscle group.
- It mainly develops the erector spinae and the glutes.
- A back can develop very well without the classic deadlift.
- Useful variations: stiff-leg deadlift (hamstrings), trap bar deadlift (glutes).

## Erector spinae

- Deadlift and bent-over rows already work the erector spinae.
- To target them specifically: hyperextensions, modified for spinal erectors (spine flexion then extension). Source's view: very effective and pleasant, feels like decompression, usually at the end of the back session.
- Rack pull: lower priority (source's view). Heavy loads compress the spine and ego pushes the load up (see the `training-principles` skill); more effective and safer options exist for the erectors.
- Hyperextensions also work glutes/hamstrings (no dedicated skill in the plugin).

## Minimal lat program

| Exercise | Action | Sets |
|---|---|---|
| Cable lat pull (unilateral, seated) | Retropulsion + adduction, balanced curve | 3-4 |
| Pronated-grip pull-ups | Adduction, bodyweight | 3-4 |
| Supinated-grip lat pulldown (optional) | Retropulsion, high stretch tension | 3-4 |

- Exercise order within a session: see `training-programming`, "Exercise order within a session (source's view)".
